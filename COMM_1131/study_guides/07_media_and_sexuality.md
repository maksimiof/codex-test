# Study Guide 7 — Media, Sexuality, and Representation

## 1. How Media Shapes Sexual Norms

Media is one of the most powerful agents of **sexual socialization** — the process by which people learn sexual norms, behaviors, and expectations.

**Sources of sexual content in media:**
- TV shows and films
- Music and music videos
- Advertising
- Social media (Instagram, TikTok, Twitter/X)
- Pornography
- Video games

---

## 2. Key Theoretical Frameworks

### Cultivation Theory — George Gerbner (1970s)
**Core Idea:** Heavy television viewing cultivates a perception of the world that reflects TV's version of reality.

- Heavy viewers tend to believe the "TV world" is more accurate than it is
- Applied to sexuality: Heavy consumers of sexualized media may overestimate promiscuity, underestimate relationship satisfaction norms
- **Mean World Syndrome:** Overestimation of danger in the world (applied to media violence, but parallels exist for sexuality)

---

### Social Learning Theory — Bandura
**Core Idea:** We learn behaviors (including sexual ones) by **observing and modeling** others, including media figures.

- We observe behavior → evaluate consequences → imitate if rewarded
- Media characters model sexual scripts that viewers may internalize and replicate

---

### Sexual Script Theory — Simon & Gagnon (1973)
**Core Idea:** Sexual behavior is scripted by culture — we learn "scripts" for how sex is supposed to happen.

**Three Levels of Scripts:**
1. **Cultural scenarios:** Society-wide norms (e.g., "men initiate, women gatekeep")
2. **Interpersonal scripts:** How scripts play out in actual interactions
3. **Intrapsychic scripts:** Internalized, personal sexual fantasies and meanings

Media reinforces and distributes **cultural scenarios**.

---

### Objectification Theory — Fredrickson & Roberts (1997)
**Core Idea:** Women's bodies are frequently treated as objects for others' visual pleasure → women internalize the observer's perspective ("self-objectification").

**Consequences of self-objectification:**
- Body shame and appearance anxiety
- Disrupted flow and concentration
- Reduced sexual enjoyment ("spectatoring")
- Depression, eating disorders

**Spectatoring:** Mentally stepping outside one's body during sex to monitor appearance → reduces presence and satisfaction.

## 3. Representation Issues in Media

### Heteronormativity in Media
- Heterosexual relationships as the default/norm
- LGBTQ+ characters often marginalized, stereotyped, or killed off ("Bury Your Gays" trope)
- Improvements: *Pose*, *Orange is the New Black*, *Schitt's Creek*, *Sex Education*

### Racial and Ethnic Stereotyping
- Hypersexualization of Black and Latina women
- Desexualization of Asian men and women
- Native and Indigenous peoples often erased or fetishized

### Body Image and the "Beauty Ideal"
- Media perpetuates thin ideal (for women) and muscular ideal (for men)
- Contributes to body dissatisfaction, dieting, and eating disorders
- Filters and editing on social media intensify this effect

### Ageism and Sexuality
- Older adults are largely desexualized in media
- Ignores real patterns of sexual activity across the lifespan

## 4. Pornography

### Research Findings (contested and evolving):
- **Consumption:** Majority of men and significant portion of women view pornography
- **Potential effects:** May shape unrealistic expectations about bodies, performance, and consent
- **Addiction debate:** "Pornography addiction" is not formally recognized in DSM-5; research is mixed
- **Positive framing for some:** Can be a tool for sexual self-discovery if critically consumed

### Feminist Debates on Pornography:
- **Anti-pornography feminists (MacKinnon, Dworkin):** Pornography is inherently exploitative; eroticizes women's subordination
- **Sex-positive feminists:** Pornography can be produced ethically; attacking porn infantilizes women's agency
- **Middle ground:** Context matters — distinguish ethical/consensual production from exploitation

## 5. Advertising and Sexuality

**"Sex Sells" — How advertising uses sexuality:**
- Sexualized images attract attention and are memorable
- Creates aspirational associations between products and desirability
- Disproportionately sexualizes women vs. men
- Contributes to objectification and unrealistic beauty standards

**Counterexamples / Social Progress:**
- Dove's Real Beauty Campaign
- Body-positive advertising movements
- Criticism of these as "femvertising" (corporate co-optation of feminism)

---

## 6. Social Media and Relationships

### Benefits:
- Facilitates maintaining long-distance relationships
- Community formation for LGBTQ+ individuals
- Access to sexual health information

### Risks and Challenges:
- **Social comparison:** Curated highlight reels fuel inadequacy
- **Parasocial relationships:** One-sided emotional attachments to media figures
- **Phubbing:** Phone-snubbing during in-person interactions; damages relationship quality
- **Digital jealousy:** Monitoring partner's activity; surveillance behavior
- **Sexting:** Risks include non-consensual sharing ("revenge porn")

## 7. Media Literacy Framework

**Being a critical media consumer means:**
1. **Identify** the source and intent of the message
2. **Deconstruct** the values, assumptions, and representations
3. **Evaluate** what is missing or marginalized
4. **Understand** the potential impact on attitudes and behavior
5. **Create** your own counter-narratives when needed

**Questions to ask about any sexual media content:**
- Who created this, and why?
- What sexual scripts does this reinforce?
- Who is represented positively? Negatively? Who is absent?
- What does this teach viewers about consent, relationships, and bodies?
