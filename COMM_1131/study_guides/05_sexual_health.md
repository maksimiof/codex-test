## 1. Defining Sexual Health

**WHO Definition:**
> "A state of physical, emotional, mental, and social well-being in relation to sexuality; it is not merely the absence of disease, dysfunction, or infirmity."

**Five Key Elements of Sexual Health:**
1. Access to accurate sexual health information
2. Ability to explore and understand one's own sexuality
3. Positive, consensual sexual relationships
4. Freedom from coercion and violence
5. Access to healthcare for sexual health conditions

## 2. Anatomy and Physiology Review

### ⚕️ Masters & Johnson Sexual Response Cycle (4 phases)

| Phase | Male | Female |
|-------|------|--------|
| **Excitement** | Penile erection; scrotal thickening | Vaginal lubrication; clitoral swelling; labia minora engorgement |
| **Plateau** | Full erection; pre-ejaculatory fluid; testicular elevation | Continued engorgement; orgasmic platform forms; Bartholin's gland secretion |
| **Orgasm** | Ejaculation (emission + expulsion); involuntary rhythmic contractions | Rhythmic contractions of uterus, vagina, pelvic floor |
| **Resolution** | Refractory period; return to unaroused state | No refractory period; return to unaroused state |

**Neurotransmitter/Hormonal Basis:** *(connects to PHSC 2320 Biochemistry)*
- **Arousal:** Parasympathetic nervous system → nitric oxide (NO) → vasodilation
- **Orgasm:** Sympathetic nervous system (emission); somatic motor (expulsion)
- **Bonding:** Oxytocin (hypothalamus) released during orgasm and touch
- **Attraction:** Dopamine (reward), norepinephrine (arousal), serotonin
- **Testosterone:** Key driver of libido in all sexes

### ⚕️ Kaplan's Three-Phase Model
An alternative model with a crucial addition:
1. **Desire** (libido) — *psychological component* (not in Masters & Johnson)
2. **Arousal** (excitement/plateau)
3. **Orgasm**

> Kaplan's model is more clinically useful because many sexual dysfunctions involve the *desire* phase.

## 3. Contraception

### Effectiveness Comparison Table

| Method | Typical Use Failure Rate | Perfect Use Failure Rate | Type |
|--------|-------------------------|--------------------------|------|
| **No contraception** | 85% | — | — |
| **Male condom** | 13% | 2% | Barrier |
| **Female condom** | 21% | 5% | Barrier |
| **Combined oral contraceptive (OCP)** | 7% | <1% | Hormonal |
| **Progestin-only pill (POP)** | 7% | <1% | Hormonal |
| **Patch (Xulane)** | 7% | <1% | Hormonal |
| **Vaginal ring (NuvaRing)** | 7% | <1% | Hormonal |
| **Depot medroxyprogesterone (Depo-Provera)** | 4% | <1% | Hormonal (injectable) |
| **Hormonal IUD** | <1% | <1% | Hormonal (LARC) |
| **Copper IUD (Paragard)** | <1% | <1% | Non-hormonal (LARC) |
| **Implant (Nexplanon)** | <1% | <1% | Hormonal (LARC) |
| **Tubal ligation** | <1% | <1% | Permanent |
| **Vasectomy** | <1% | <1% | Permanent |
| **FAM (symptothermal)** | 12–24% | 0.4% | Behavioral |

**LARC = Long-Acting Reversible Contraception**

### Mechanism of Action: Hormonal Contraceptives *(PHSC 2320 connection)*
- **Estrogen/Progestin combinations:** Suppress GnRH → ↓ FSH, LH → prevent ovulation; thicken cervical mucus; thin endometrium
- **Progestin-only:** Primarily thicken cervical mucus; may suppress ovulation inconsistently
- **Copper IUD:** Copper ions are spermicidal; inflammatory reaction prevents implantation

### Emergency Contraception (EC)
| Product | Mechanism | Window |
|---------|-----------|--------|
| **Plan B One-Step® (levonorgestrel)** | Delays/prevents ovulation; does NOT cause abortion | Within 72 hours (effective up to 120h) |
| **Ella® (ulipristal acetate)** | Progesterone receptor modulator; delays ovulation | Within 120 hours |
| **Copper IUD** | Most effective EC; prevents implantation | Within 120 hours |

⚕️ *Pharmacist Note: Plan B is OTC; no age restriction. Ella requires prescription. Counsel patients on timing and effectiveness.*

## 4. Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)

### Overview Table

| STI | Pathogen | Transmission | Symptoms | Treatment |
|-----|---------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| **Chlamydia** | *C. trachomatis* (bacteria) | Vaginal, anal, oral sex | Often asymptomatic; discharge, burning | Azithromycin or doxycycline |
| **Gonorrhea** | *N. gonorrhoeae* (bacteria) | Vaginal, anal, oral sex | Discharge, dysuria; often asymptomatic | Ceftriaxone (IM) |
| **Syphilis** | *T. pallidum* (bacteria) | Skin-to-skin contact, blood | Painless chancre (primary); rash (secondary); systemic (tertiary) | Benzathine penicillin G |
| **Herpes (HSV-1, HSV-2)** | Herpes simplex virus | Skin-to-skin, oral contact | Painful blisters; often asymptomatic | Acyclovir, valacyclovir (suppressive) |
| **HPV** | Human papillomavirus | Skin-to-skin | Genital warts; cervical/other cancers | Warts: topical/procedural; Vaccine: Gardasil 9® |
| **HIV** | Human immunodeficiency virus | Blood, semen, vaginal fluids, breast milk | Acute flu-like; chronic immune decline; AIDS | ART (antiretroviral therapy) — not a cure |
| **Trichomoniasis** | *T. vaginalis* (protozoan) | Sexual contact | Itching, burning, frothy discharge | Metronidazole or tinidazole |
| **Hepatitis B** | Hepatitis B virus | Blood, sexual fluids | Fatigue, jaundice, liver disease | Vaccine-preventable; antivirals for chronic |

### HIV — Deeper Dive *(PharmD relevance)*

**PrEP (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis):**
- **Truvada® (emtricitabine/tenofovir DF):** Daily oral pill
- **Descovy® (emtricitabine/tenofovir AF):** For cisgender men and transgender women
- **Apretude® (cabotegravir IM):** Bimonthly injection
- Reduces HIV transmission risk by up to 99% when taken correctly
- ⚕️ Pharmacists can initiate PrEP in some states; always counsel on adherence

**PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis):**
- Must be started within **72 hours** of exposure
- 28-day course of antiretrovirals
- ⚕️ Pharmacists may stock PEP starter packs

**Testing Frequency Recommendations (CDC):**
- General adults: at least once in lifetime
- Sexually active gay/bisexual men: every 3–6 months
- Anyone with new/multiple partners: at least annually
- Pregnant people: first prenatal visit

## 5. Sexual Dysfunction

| Dysfunction | Description | Potential Causes |
|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Erectile dysfunction (ED)** | Inability to achieve/maintain erection sufficient for sex | Cardiovascular disease, diabetes, medications (β-blockers, SSRIs), psychological |
| **Premature ejaculation** | Ejaculation that occurs sooner than desired | Psychological, neurological |
| **Female sexual interest/arousal disorder** | Reduced interest/arousal causing distress | Hormonal (menopause), medications, psychological |
| **Genitopelvic pain/penetration disorder** | Pain with penetration | Vaginismus, endometriosis, trauma |
| **Orgasmic disorder** | Inability to achieve orgasm | Psychological, medications (SSRIs), insufficient stimulation |

⚕️ *Pharmacist Note: SSRIs, antihypertensives, antiandrogens, and many other drug classes cause sexual dysfunction as a side effect — patient counseling is critical.*

## 6. Reproductive Health Across the Lifespan

| Life Stage | Key Issues |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Adolescence** | Puberty; first sexual experiences; contraceptive access |
| **Reproductive years** | Contraception; fertility; STI prevention |
| **Pregnancy** | Medication safety (category A–D, X); folic acid counseling |
| **Perimenopause/Menopause** | Declining estrogen; vaginal atrophy; HRT considerations |
| **Older adulthood** | Continued sexual activity; STI risk underestimated; ED medications |

## 7. Communicating About Sexual Health

**PLISSIT Model** (sexual health counseling framework):
- **P**ermission: Create a safe space to discuss sexual concerns
- **L**imited **I**nformation: Provide basic, relevant facts
- **SS** (Specific Suggestions): Targeted advice for specific concerns
- **IT** (Intensive Therapy): Referral for complex issues
