## 1. Intersectionality

### The Framework — Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989)
**Definition:** A framework for understanding how multiple social identities (race, gender, class, sexuality, disability, religion, nationality) overlap and interact to create unique experiences of privilege and oppression.

**Origin:** Crenshaw coined the term to describe how Black women's experiences couldn't be captured by looking at race *or* gender alone — only by looking at both *together*.

**Key Insights:**
- Social categories are not additive ("being a woman + being Black") — they are multiplicative and interactive
- Oppression at the intersection is qualitatively different, not just more of the same
- Policy and advocacy that focus on single identities often fail those at intersections

**Privilege Matrix:**
Any person can be simultaneously privileged in some dimensions and marginalized in others.

| Dimension | Privileged | Marginalized |
|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| Race | White | People of color |
| Gender | Men (cisgender) | Women, nonbinary |
| Sexuality | Heterosexual | LGBTQ+ |
| Class | Upper/middle | Working class, poor |
| Disability | Able-bodied | Disabled |
| Religion | Christian (in U.S.) | Non-Christian, non-religious |

## 2. Cultural Influences on Sexuality and Relationships

### Individualism vs. Collectivism
- **Individualistic cultures** (U.S., Western Europe): Personal choice, romantic love, individual fulfillment as primary basis of relationships
- **Collectivistic cultures** (many South/East Asian, Middle Eastern, African societies): Family and community approval; practical considerations; arranged marriages more common

### Arranged vs. Love Marriages
- **Arranged marriage** does not necessarily mean forced marriage
- Research shows arranged marriages can produce high satisfaction — often love develops *after* commitment
- Forced marriage (no consent) is distinct and ethically problematic

### Sexual Norms Across Cultures
- Age of consent varies by country (range: 13–21)
- Homosexuality criminalized in ~65+ countries; punishable by death in some
- Premarital sex varies in social acceptance across cultures
- Polyandry (one woman, multiple husbands) practiced in parts of Tibet, Nepal

## 3. Race, Ethnicity, and Sexuality in the U.S.

### Hypersexualization and Desexualization
- **Black women:** Historically hypersexualized (Jezebel stereotype) and desexualized (Mammy stereotype)
- **Black men:** Hypersexualized; "dangerous" stereotypes; intersects with police violence
- **Latina women:** Hypersexualized ("spicy Latina" trope)
- **Asian women:** Fetishized/exoticized (Lotus Blossom/Dragon Lady)
- **Asian men:** Desexualized; emasculated in media

**Impact:** These stereotypes affect self-perception, dating experiences, healthcare interactions, and mental health.

### Health Disparities
- Black women: highest maternal mortality rate in the U.S. (3–4x white women)
- Black and Hispanic individuals: higher STI rates (due to structural inequities, not behavior)
- LGBTQ+ youth of color: higher rates of homelessness and mental health issues

## 4. Disability and Sexuality

**Common Myths (and the reality):**
- ❌ "Disabled people don't have sex" → People with disabilities have sexual desires and relationships
- ❌ "Disability is always a barrier to sex" → Sexual expression is highly adaptable
- ❌ "Disabled people should be asexual for simplicity" → This is ableist

**Sexual Rights of People with Disabilities:**
- Right to sexual expression
- Right to sexual health information in accessible formats
- Right to privacy
- Right to not be sexually exploited

**Pharmacy context:** Medication interactions, adaptive devices, assistive communication — pharmacists serve many patients with disabilities.

## 5. Religion, Spirituality, and Sexuality

| Tradition | Common Teaching | Range of Interpretation |
|-----------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Christianity** | Sex within heterosexual marriage | Fundamentalist → affirming LGBT churches |
| **Islam** | Sex within heterosexual marriage; homosexuality prohibited | Conservative → progressive |
| **Judaism** | Generally sex-positive within marriage | Orthodox → Reform acceptance of LGBTQ+ |
| **Hinduism** | Varied; historical acceptance of third gender | Conservative → liberal |
| **Buddhism** | Non-harm principle; relatively non-prescriptive | Varies by tradition |

**Key Point:** Religion is not monolithic — there is enormous diversity within any tradition. Avoid stereotypes.

**Relevance:** Religious beliefs deeply influence sexual decision-making, contraceptive choices, and end-of-life care.

## 6. Age and Sexuality

### Adolescent Sexuality
- Sexual development begins with puberty (hormonal changes: estrogen, testosterone, GnRH)
- Identity exploration; sexual debut
- Risk factors: lack of comprehensive sex education; peer pressure; partner age gaps

### Adult Sexuality
- Sexual satisfaction generally stable across adulthood with healthy relationships
- Stress, children, career can reduce frequency but not necessarily satisfaction

### Sexuality in Older Adults
- Sexual activity continues into late life for many
- Physiological changes: vaginal atrophy (estrogen ↓), erectile changes, slower arousal
- STI risk often underestimated by providers; older adults receive less STI counseling

⚕️ *Pharmacist note: Never assume an elderly patient doesn't need STI counseling or contraceptive information.*

## 7. Trauma-Informed Approaches

### Sexual Trauma and Its Sequelae
- Sexual assault, childhood sexual abuse, IPV, and reproductive coercion can have lasting effects
- **PTSD** (re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal, negative cognition)
- **Sexual dysfunction** — pain, arousal disorder, avoidance
- **Relationship difficulties** — trust, intimacy, communication

### Trauma-Informed Care Principles (SAMHSA):
1. **Safety**
2. **Trustworthiness and transparency**
3. **Peer support**
4. **Collaboration and mutuality**
5. **Empowerment, voice, and choice**
6. **Cultural, historical, and gender issues** addressed

### What This Looks Like in Communication:
- Normalize sensitive questions before asking ("I ask all patients...")
- Offer choice and control at every step
- Avoid asking "why" questions (can feel accusatory)
- Believe the patient; don't interrogate their account

## 8. LGBTQ+ Health Disparities

| Population | Health Disparity |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Gay/bisexual men** | Higher HIV/STI rates; depression; substance use |
| **Lesbian/bisexual women** | Lower rates of cancer screening; higher obesity rates |
| **Trans individuals** | Mental health crises; discrimination in healthcare; higher HIV risk |
| **LGBTQ+ youth** | Higher rates of homelessness; suicide (4× general population) |
| **LGBTQ+ elders** | Social isolation; discrimination in long-term care |

**Minority Stress Model (Meyer, 2003):**
Chronic stress from stigma, discrimination, and prejudice explains health disparities in LGBTQ+ populations beyond lifestyle factors.
