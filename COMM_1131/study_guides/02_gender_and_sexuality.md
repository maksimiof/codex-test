# Study Guide 2 — Gender, Sexuality, and Identity

---

## 1. Key Distinctions

| Term | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Sex** | Biological classification (chromosomes, hormones, anatomy) | Male, female, intersex |
| **Gender** | Social/psychological identity; a continuum | Man, woman, nonbinary, genderfluid |
| **Gender Expression** | External presentation | Clothing, hairstyle, mannerisms |
| **Sexual Orientation** | Pattern of attraction (emotional, romantic, sexual) | Heterosexual, gay, bisexual, asexual |

> **Key Point:** Sex ≠ Gender ≠ Sexual Orientation — they are independent dimensions.

---

## 2. Theoretical Frameworks

### Social Constructionism
Gender and sexuality are not biological givens but are *constructed* through social processes, cultural norms, interaction, and language.

- Judith Butler (*Gender Trouble*, 1990): gender is **performative** — it exists through repeated acts, not as an essential quality
- "Doing gender" (West & Zimmerman, 1987): gender is *accomplished* in everyday interactions

### Essentialism vs. Constructionism

| Essentialism | Constructionism |
|-------------|----------------|
| Gender/sexuality rooted in biology/nature | Gender/sexuality rooted in culture/nurture |
| Fixed, universal categories | Fluid, historically specific categories |
| Example: "Men are naturally more aggressive" | Example: "Masculinity norms vary by culture" |

> Neither extreme is fully accurate; current science recognizes **biopsychosocial** interaction.

## 3. Gender Identity Spectrum

```
Binary                         Non-Binary
Man ←————————————————————————————————→ Woman
              ↕
       Genderqueer / Nonbinary
       Genderfluid
       Agender
```

**Important Terms:**
- **Cisgender:** Gender identity matches sex assigned at birth
- **Transgender:** Gender identity differs from sex assigned at birth
- **Nonbinary/Genderqueer:** Does not identify exclusively as man or woman
- **Genderfluid:** Gender identity shifts over time or context
- **Agender:** No gender identity, or gender-neutral

## 4. Sexual Orientation Models

### The Kinsey Scale (1948)
Alfred Kinsey proposed that sexual orientation exists on a **continuum**:

```
0 — Exclusively heterosexual
1 — Predominantly heterosexual, only incidentally homosexual
2 — Predominantly heterosexual, but more than incidentally homosexual
3 — Equally heterosexual and homosexual (bisexual)
4 — Predominantly homosexual, but more than incidentally heterosexual
5 — Predominantly homosexual, only incidentally heterosexual
6 — Exclusively homosexual
X — No socio-sexual contacts or reactions (asexual)
```

> **Criticism:** Still binary (only male/female attractions); doesn't capture romantic vs. sexual vs. emotional attraction separately.

### The Klein Sexual Orientation Grid (KSOG)
Expands Kinsey by assessing **seven dimensions** across past, present, and ideal:
1. Sexual attraction
2. Sexual behavior
3. Sexual fantasies
4. Emotional preference
5. Social preference
6. Lifestyle preference
7. Self-identification

### The Split Attraction Model
Separates sexual attraction from **romantic attraction** and other forms:
- **Aromantic** (little/no romantic attraction) ≠ Asexual (little/no sexual attraction)
- Example: Romantic heterosexual + asexual = heteroromantic asexual

## 5. LGBTQ+ Identities Overview

| Identity | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Lesbian** | Woman attracted to women |
| **Gay** | Man attracted to men (or umbrella term) |
| **Bisexual** | Attracted to own and other genders |
| **Transgender** | Gender identity differs from assigned sex |
| **Queer** | Umbrella; may reject conventional categories |
| **Questioning** | Exploring identity |
| **Intersex** | Biological sex characteristics outside typical definitions |
| **Asexual** | Little/no sexual attraction |
| **Pansexual** | Attracted regardless of gender |
| **Nonbinary** | Gender outside man/woman binary |

## 6. Coming Out as a Process

Coming out is **not a single event** but an ongoing, context-dependent process.

**Cass Identity Model (1979) — Six stages:**
1. **Identity Confusion:** "Am I gay?"
2. **Identity Comparison:** "Maybe this applies to me"
3. **Identity Tolerance:** Seeks out LGBTQ+ community
4. **Identity Acceptance:** Positive self-view; selective disclosure
5. **Identity Pride:** Strong community identification; "us vs. them"
6. **Identity Synthesis:** LGBTQ+ identity integrates into whole self

**Considerations:**
- Coming out can be affirming and liberating
- Also carries real risks (rejection, discrimination, violence)
- Intersects with race, class, religion, culture
- People come out at different life stages

## 7. Feminist Perspectives on Gender

### Liberal Feminism
Focus: Equal rights and opportunities through legal reform; dismantling formal barriers.

### Radical Feminism
Focus: Patriarchy is the root cause of women's oppression; requires fundamental restructuring of society.

### Intersectional Feminism (Kimberlé Crenshaw)
Focus: Gender cannot be analyzed in isolation — it intersects with race, class, sexuality, disability, and other identities.

### Queer Theory (Butler, Foucault)
Focus: Deconstructs all fixed categories of gender and sexuality; challenges heteronormativity and the binary.

## 8. Cultural Variations in Gender & Sexuality

- **Two-Spirit (Indigenous cultures):** A third gender recognized in many Indigenous North American cultures
- **Hijra (South Asia):** Third gender with long cultural/spiritual history in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh
- **Fa'afafine (Samoa):** A third gender identity accepted in Samoan culture
- Cultural context critically shapes what identities are recognized, valued, or stigmatized
