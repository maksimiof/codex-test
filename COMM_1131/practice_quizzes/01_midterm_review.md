## Section 2: Short Answer

**11. Explain the difference between the Shannon-Weaver Transmission Model and the Transactional Model of communication. Which better captures real relationship communication and why?**

> **Model Answer:** The Shannon-Weaver model is *linear* — a sender encodes a message, sends it through a channel, and a receiver decodes it; feedback is a later addition. The Transactional model holds that both parties are *simultaneously* senders and receivers, constantly influencing each other; communication is dynamic, ongoing, and co-created within shared contexts. The Transactional model better reflects real relationships because we are always sending and receiving signals (verbal, nonverbal, emotional) at the same time — a simple linear model can't capture this complexity.

**12. What is "depenetration" in Social Penetration Theory? Give a specific example.**

> **Model Answer:** Depenetration is the withdrawal of self-disclosure as a relationship deteriorates. Just as relationships deepen through increased breadth and depth of sharing, they dissolve as sharing becomes more restricted and shallow. *Example:* A couple going through a breakup gradually stops sharing vulnerable information — they move from discussing fears and dreams to discussing only logistics, then eventually avoiding meaningful conversation altogether.

**13. Distinguish between "sex," "gender," and "sexual orientation." Why is maintaining this distinction important for healthcare professionals?**

> **Model Answer:** *Sex* refers to biological characteristics (chromosomes, hormones, anatomy). *Gender* is a person's internal identity and social expression — it exists on a spectrum and may not match assigned sex. *Sexual orientation* describes the pattern of emotional/romantic/sexual attraction and is independent of both sex and gender. These are three separate dimensions. For healthcare professionals, conflating them leads to incorrect assumptions (e.g., assuming a transgender man is attracted to women) that can create unsafe, non-affirming care. Accurate patient care requires asking respectfully and not assuming.

**14. What are the three primary strategies for reducing uncertainty according to Berger & Calabrese? Give an example of each.**

> **Model Answer:**
> 1. **Passive** — Observing a person in their natural environment without interaction. *Example:* Watching how someone interacts with others at a party before deciding to approach.
> 2. **Active** — Seeking information about someone from third parties. *Example:* Asking a mutual friend what a person is like.
> 3. **Interactive** — Direct conversation with the person. *Example:* Asking questions, sharing information to elicit reciprocal disclosure.

**15. What is "contempt" in Gottman's Four Horsemen, and why is it the most destructive of the four?**

> **Model Answer:** Contempt involves communicating superiority, disrespect, or disgust toward a partner — through mockery, sarcasm, eye-rolling, name-calling. Unlike criticism (which attacks behavior), contempt attacks the person's fundamental worth. Gottman identified it as the single best predictor of divorce because it signals fundamental disrespect, undermines any sense of goodwill, and makes repair nearly impossible. It creates a hostile communication climate where the partner feels worthless rather than merely criticized.

## Section 3: Application / Scenario Questions

**16. A patient comes to the pharmacy counter and quietly asks you about emergency contraception. They seem embarrassed. Using the PLISSIT model, describe how you would approach this interaction.**

> **Model Answer:**
> - **P (Permission):** Create a private, judgment-free space. Say: "I'm glad you came to me — this is exactly what I'm here for. Let me step to a more private area with you." This signals that it is safe and acceptable to discuss.
> - **LI (Limited Information):** Provide accurate, non-judgmental basic facts: "Plan B is available without a prescription. It works best within 72 hours but can be effective up to 120 hours after unprotected sex. It prevents pregnancy by delaying or preventing ovulation — it is not an abortion pill."
> - **SS (Specific Suggestions):** "Given the timing, I'd recommend Plan B One-Step. Take it as soon as possible. If it's been more than 72 hours, I can discuss other options like Ella."
> - **IT (Intensive Therapy):** If the patient raises concerns about a sexual assault or ongoing relationship issues, offer referral: "If you'd like to talk to someone about what happened, I can connect you with resources."

**17. Two partners, Alex and Jordan, argue frequently about how much time Jordan spends with friends. Alex feels abandoned; Jordan feels controlled. Using Relational Dialectics Theory, explain this conflict and suggest a management strategy.**

> **Model Answer:** This is a classic **Autonomy–Connection** dialectic. Jordan values autonomy (independent social life), while Alex values connection (spending time together). Neither pole is "wrong" — both are legitimate needs. Importantly, this tension cannot be permanently "solved" because both needs are valid and ongoing. A productive management strategy is **cyclic alternation** — they could agree that certain weeknights or weekends are reserved for the couple, while other times Jordan has freedom to see friends. Alternatively, **segmentation** could work: Jordan's Thursday nights are friend nights; Saturday evenings are couple time. This validates both needs without declaring a winner.

**18. Apply the concept of intersectionality to explain why health disparities around STIs cannot be explained by "risky behavior" alone.**

> **Model Answer:** Crenshaw's intersectionality framework shows that outcomes are shaped by multiple, overlapping social factors. Higher STI rates in some racial/ethnic groups are not explained by differential sexual behavior but by structural factors: lack of healthcare access due to poverty, healthcare system distrust rooted in historical racism (e.g., Tuskegee), neighborhoods with higher STI prevalence (assortative mixing), discrimination limiting health literacy, and limited access to insurance. A Black, low-income, uninsured woman faces barriers to STI testing and treatment that a white, middle-class, insured woman does not — regardless of comparable behavior. Attributing disparities solely to behavior individualizes what are structurally caused problems.
